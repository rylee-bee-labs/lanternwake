title: A Book About Books

A small volume about a large habit.

Who writes them: anyone with a reason and a piece of something that will hold ink. Charters are written by whoever wants the rule to outlast them. Repair logs by whoever has the nails. Sermons by the one who will be standing at the rail. Songs by nobody — songs are kept, and altered, and kept again, which is a slower kind of writing.

The coast's books fall into two piles. Books of work — ledgers, lists, warnings, tide marks — and books of weather: letters, sermons, songs, the almanac. Both piles are trying to do the same thing. One says how much. The other says how it was.

* * *

Who shelves them: the Guild's clerk while there was a Guildhall, then anybody with a dry shelf. The inn keeps a row above the fire. The chandlery keeps the salt off them. The chapel keeps what the damp has not taken. The Beacon house keeps the best in a tin box, a wool blanket over.

Who reads them aloud: whoever can, to whoever cannot, which here is nearly the same list twice. Children are read to at the hearth, the old at the rail. Travellers are read the warning board at the slip, in case they walk after dark — and they walk earlier, which was always the board's point.

* * *

The argument, since a book that will not argue is only a shelf's worth of paper:

A book is a lamp with pages.

It is lit by somebody else's hand and it holds while you are near it. It does not go out when the wind gets up. It takes oil — attention, a cold evening, a finger holding the line — and it is no good to a man walking past in a hurry.

A lamp keeps a place named. A book keeps a name lit. The Guild burned, the lamps went out, and the coast went on reading, which is proof enough that the second is older than the first.

Keep them dry. Read them to somebody.
