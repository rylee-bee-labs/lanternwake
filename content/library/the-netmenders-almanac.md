title: The Netmender's Almanac

A year of mending, kept at the racks. Spring: the winter nets come in shredded at the cod-end, every year, and every year I swear I will dock them deeper. Twine rots first where it rides wet, which is the lesson, and never stops being the lesson. Summer: cuts, mostly, and the odd gaff that went through a screen like a knife through smoke. What breaks is seldom the big part. It is the crossing strand you did not look at in March, and it waits, and it tells you so in October.

* * *

What holds: the knots do. A sheet bend done properly outlives the net, the boat, and the man who tied it. I have a mesh in the corner rack off my grandmother's line; it has been cut four times and it still holds a shape of its own, like a hand that has done one work all its life. Nothing in this stall has been new since the hall burned. Everything in it works. I would like people to notice that, though I do not go about saying it.

* * *

What comes back: every gale leaves the racks a gift and I sort it by lamplight. Rope, floats, half a crate, once a boot worth mending. And things that are not mine to keep, which go in the box by the door for whoever lost them. My brother's cap came back that way eleven days after, and I have it still, and I do not wear it. The sea returns everything it takes, on its own terms, at its own hour. I set the box out all the same. That is the work.

* * *

On grief. A net that has been torn is not spoiled. Take it up, find the edge of the hole, work back from the standing part, mesh for mesh, until the hole is gone and there is a join where there used to be a gap. It will be the strongest line in the net, and your thumb will know where it is for years. It is not the same net it was. I would not sell it to you as new, and I have stopped wanting it new. It holds. That is what a net is for. That is the whole of the almanac.
