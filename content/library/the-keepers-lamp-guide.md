Printed by the Guild of the Wake for those going out for the first time. On wicks: trim to a finger's width, trim it square, and trim it again the day after tomorrow whether it seems to need it or not. A burnt wick makes a small mean light that eats its own glass. Cut the black off with a knife, never with your nails. A good flame is a low dome with a blue heart and no smoke to speak of. If it smokes, the lamp is telling you something. Listen to the lamp. It has not once lied to anybody.

* * *

On weather: read the water, not the sky. Flat calm with the gulls sitting on it means a blow before midnight. A ring round the moon is a rope, and ropes tighten. If the wind comes up the road instead of down it, shut the shutter and walk home; a light fought is a light wasted. Salt on the glass by morning means the air is turning. In a gale keep the flame small. A small flame that stands is worth more than a proud one gone out, and it will still be there when the gale remembers where it was going.

* * *

On glass: a cracked pane is not a broken lamp. Take the frame down, wet the string, and press the crack so it drinks. The lead will hold it a season, which is often all a season asks of anyone. For a hole, cut a square out of the spare, overlap it a thumb, and blacken the seam with tallow so you can see your own work. Keep the spare behind the door where the draught will not find it, and never carry glass in the same pocket as the keys.

* * *

On kindness, which is last here only because it is the first job. A lamp is not lit for the road; the road does not care. It is lit for whoever is on it. Keep the seat by the fire for the one who comes in last. Say the name of the person the Hush took, out loud, once a week, so the word does not go soft with disuse. Mend the thing before it is asked for. This is maintenance, and it is no different from trimming a wick. A coast that keeps at it will keep its name. One page a day, and it will.
