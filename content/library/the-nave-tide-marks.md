The tide marks cut into the nave's pillars. The third pillar has the best of them, and the oldest.

1740 — a notch, and under it a second line for the tide that followed the first one home.

1750 — a hand's breadth lower. Whoever cut it was easy that year; the line is clean and unhurried.

1760 — lower still.

1770 — level with the first. Beneath it, faint: so it begins again.

1780 — two fingers above the first notch.

1790 — three.

Then the cutters stopped being patient. 1791, 1792, 1793, each a shade higher, each the same careful chisel — one hand, winter after winter, in a nave that stood dry.

* * *

The last ten years are on the fourth pillar, because the third ran out of stone above the salt line.

Year of the first Dim — a thumb above the old record. Year two — a little more. Year three the cutter stopped midway, the chisel slipped, and there is a short wrong line beside a patient one.

Years four through ten sit higher and closer together, cut by the same hand at the same height each time, which means the sea is not so much higher every year as further in.

Between them, small and shallow and not cut with a chisel at all, a name: SEF. Someone has been recording it. Someone is still here to record.
