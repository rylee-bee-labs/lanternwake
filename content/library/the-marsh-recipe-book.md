Brine chowder, for coming in out of the wet. Water off the pans, a handful of shingle-crab, an onion, and salt to taste — always less than you think, for it is brine already. Set it off the fire while you take your boots off. Put a bowl by the door before you sit down, because someone is always coming, and hand it over before he has finished saying he is not hungry. It is the bowl that warms them, mostly, and the chair you pull out while it is still yours to give.

* * *

Marsh tea, for an argument that has gone on too long. Take the grey leaf that grows above the waterline and never a leaf from below it. Boil once, pour, and hold your tongue while it steams; the steam does the listening. Wren drinks hers scalding and calls it her patience. Sable puts honey in hers and falls asleep in the middle of being told off. Both are sound methods. Second cup is for the one who spoke last.

* * *

Smoked eel, for the long walk tomorrow. Split and salt at dusk, hang it on the hook above the fire where the smoke is lazy rather than hot. Turn it once before you sleep. It keeps a fortnight in the cold and a week in a warm hand. Grandmother Ash says the eel remembers the marsh and tastes of it. She says a great many things and is right about this. Send the bones down to the dogs and eat it standing at the door with the salt on your fingers.

* * *

Blackberry cordial, for a house that has lost somebody. Take the last brambles of the year, the ones the birds have passed over. Sugar, a scrap of lemon if the boat came in, and one bark of cinnamon if Hobb has any spare — he always has some spare and will not take payment for it. Boil it down dark and let it stand overnight in the cold. Keep it for the knock at the door you are not expecting. A full shelf of this is the coast's way of saying, sit down.
