Spring. The lamp clearing is a black ring where the Weald Lamp stood, and the pines nearest it lean in the way a crowd leans over a fire. I have measured two of them against the stake and they have moved a hand's width since the frost went out of the ground. Trees do not lean for nothing. The crows are at it again, carrying small bright things out of the old keeper's hut — a button, a nail, an amber bead — and hiding them north. I let them. They are tidier about it than I am.

* * *

Summer. Warm enough that the resin runs and the whole wood smells of it in the evenings. The leaning has spread past the third row; the birches have joined in, and birches are fussy about where they put their backs. Sable came through singing and every bird went quiet for exactly as long as she lasted. The Hush pools on the low ground at deep-night and took no notice of her at all. She is half right about the tune. Sing anyway.

* * *

Autumn. The crows have given up hiding things and started bringing them back. Twice this month I have found at my door: a thimble, a shard of green glass, a spoon with the bowl worn thin. Whatever they are practising, it is not theft. The wind gets into the eastern ride at dusk and moves through it like somebody walking who does not want to be heard. I have stopped calling out. Whoever it is, it has never once come nearer than the second row, and I have kept this wood eleven years.

* * *

One night in the turning of the year the wood held its breath. Every animal sound went out at once, which never happens — they take turns being afraid. The lean stopped. Even the wind came off the ride and stood in the open with nothing to say. I sat with the lamp between my knees until the sweat ran off me and I did not put it out. Nothing came. Nothing ever does. Then the owls started up again over by the ash, and the wood let it go and went about its business. I have written this twice, for the first time I did not believe myself.
